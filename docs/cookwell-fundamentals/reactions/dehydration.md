# Dehydration

Source path: `/fundamental/reaction/dehydration`

Evaporating water content from an ingredient with heat and/or air circulation.

## What are the fundamentals of dehydration?

Dehydration is the process of evaporating water content from an ingredient with heat and/or air circulation to manipulate the flavor, texture, or appeal of a food.

- Because water generally evaporates at 212°F/100°C, dehydration happens rapidly with cooking techniques that favor higher temperatures, such as [Sear](../techniques/sear.md) or [Deep Fry](../techniques/deep-fry.md), which can render a dehydrated and thus crisp surface texture.
- In other cases, dehydration can happen through convection, or the circulation of air through a space, which can wick away surface moisture and dry out an ingredient over time.

Salt, or [Brine](../techniques/brine.md), can help with dehydration since it draws out moisture to the surface of ingredients through osmosis. As salt moves inward, it pushes water outward, which can be removed more easily with air or evaporative heat.

> Catalyst: Heat, convection (air), and/or salt (osmosis)

> Relevant techniques: [Roast](../techniques/roast.md), [Deep Fry](../techniques/deep-fry.md), [Stir Fry](../techniques/stir-fry.md)

> Relevant molecules: [Water](../molecules/water.md), [Protein](../molecules/protein.md), [Carbohydrate](../molecules/carbohydrate.md)

### Example foods

- Potato chips
- Beef jerky
- Fried chicken
- Pan-seared salmon with crispy skin
- Dried spices

## How does dehydration affect the elements of flavor?

Texture - Dehydration primarily affects texture. It can be subtle, like tightening roast chicken skin or slight wrinkling on carrot or sausage exteriors. Even small dehydration matters for texture.

Human - Different cultures value different textures related to how much an ingredient is dehydrated. Jerky should be dry and chewy, a tortilla chip crunchy and not soggy, cake light and airy, and brownies fudgy and undercooked.

Sight - We assess ingredient state by sight using visual clues such as color or texture patterns. Ingredients can appear shiny and wet, or dry and wrinkly. Color can turn pale or darker and more striking.

Physical - Dehydrated foods can be harder to chew or bite through. Dried ingredients can become more concentrated and potent in physical effects.

Aroma - Dehydration itself does not produce new aromas, although it can concentrate remaining aromatic compounds. Delicate ingredients might lose most smell during dehydration because volatile aromatics degrade with heat and escape through convection.

Taste - Dehydration does not change taste by itself, but it concentrates it because less water is present.
