# Coagulation

Source path: `/fundamental/reaction/Coagulation`

The changing of protein structures with heat, salt, or acid.

## What are the fundamentals of coagulation?

Coagulation is what happens to proteins as they become fully cooked. When you expose protein-rich foods to salt, acid, or heat, they drastically change their structure, like raw vs. cooked chicken.

Protein molecules, amino acids, are shaped like coiled or folded strands. These easily unravel, or denature, when exposed to salt, acid, or light heat starting at around 140-180°F/60-80°C. As cooking processes expose these denatured proteins to higher temperatures, the uncoiled strands begin to link up with one another, resulting in coagulation, or the final cooked protein stage.

- Scrambled eggs go from raw and gloopy to thin and watery over low heat, then solid as they cook and set: their protein strands denature and then coagulate together to form curds.
- This process can be taken too far, such as overcooking a steak, where coagulated proteins become so tightly bound that they push out internal water content, leading to dry, rubbery, or chewy texture.

Different protein types, like collagen, break down in slightly different ways. When exposed to heat over a long period, collagen changes its structure into soft and tender gelatin.

How is coagulation different from [Gelation](./gelation.md)? Coagulation relies on a cooked protein network to solidify dishes, while gelation uses starch-based thickeners, like pectin.

> Catalysts: Heat, change in PH, or salt

## How does coagulation affect the elements of flavor?

Texture - While denaturing loosens protein strands, coagulation firms everything back up into a matrix. Gone too far, and the process can squeeze out water content: overcooked eggs leak water, and an overdone steak loses moisture.

Human - Denaturing then coagulating proteins with heat, salt, or acid has allowed humans to preserve and digest proteins for thousands of years. Humans can be appetized or disgusted by protein state, and generally prefer firm, cooked foods over raw or mushy proteins.

Sight - We use our eyes to determine how protein has been prepared. A medium-rare steak is first evaluated by color. A flan is checked with a jiggle or spoon to see if milk and egg proteins have coagulated enough.

Physical - Cooked or cured proteins can be easier or safer to digest than raw proteins.

Taste - Denaturing/coagulating proteins does not directly affect taste. Because it usually happens with heat, other reactions like [Maillard](./maillard.md) might happen simultaneously.

Aroma - Denaturing/coagulating proteins does not directly affect aroma, but Maillard can happen simultaneously.
